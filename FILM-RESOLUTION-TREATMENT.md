# RESOLUTION — film treatment

**A festival entry for the Higgsfield Global Film Festival. Deadline 14 Sep 2026.**

> **Logline:** Shot entirely from inside a child's eyes, from the first second of life to the first day of school — rendered exactly as the developing brain actually sees and hears it.

Runtime target **4:00** (rules require 3:00 minimum). Format 21:9. No dialogue the child cannot yet parse.

The title carries the film's whole structure. *Resolution* is what the image gains — literally, optically, over five years — and it is what the story reaches at the end.

---

## 1. Why this concept works here

Before the science, the strategic read, because it changed my mind.

**This is the single most differentiated concept available in this field.** Section 6 of the festival plan shows 3,100 entrants converging on dystopian sci-fi, horror and sombre genre drama. Nothing in that field is doing perceptual reconstruction. It is not a variation on what everyone else is making — it is a different category of object.

**It is aimed straight at this specific jury.**

| Juror | What they get |
|---|---|
| **Phedon Papamichael** (ASC, cinematographer) | A film whose entire subject is optics — acuity, contrast, focal distance, colour arriving in order. He will read this more fluently than any other entry in the competition. |
| **Edwin Catmull** (Pixar) | A wordless emotional story about a parent and a child. This is the Pixar short form exactly. |
| **Alex Proyas** | A strong formal conceit, world-built entirely through subjective perception. |
| **Paul W. S. Anderson** | The weakest fit — no kinetics. Mitigated by the birth sequence, which is a genuine sensory assault, and the first-steps sequence. |

**It maps onto all four stated shortlist criteria at once.** Visual craft *is* the premise. Sound and editing *are* the premise. Originality is uncontested. And the storytelling is a clean emotional arc rather than a tech demo — which is the trap this concept has to avoid.

**The medium's weaknesses become the film's aesthetic.** This is the part that matters most for production. Generative video fails at face consistency, temporal stability and uncanny fine detail. This film is scientifically *required* to be out of focus, low-contrast and colour-poor for its first act, and to hold on partial views of faces throughout. **The blur is not a compromise — it is the research.** Every artifact that would sink a conventional entry is invisible here, or reads as intended.

**One honest caveat.** My earlier recommendation was comedy, because comedy won the last contest outright and the field has abandoned it. This concept gives up that edge. It is a legitimate trade: it wins on originality and craft instead, and the previous jury did also reward *In Despair* ("a beautifully told, simple story with a great, unexpected twist ending") and *What A Lovely Day* ("for family values"). Heart takes mentions. But the risk is real and worth naming: **a sensory film that fails is boring, where a comedy that fails is merely unfunny.** The pacing discipline in Section 4 is what manages that.

## 2. The science

Everything below is from the research pass. The film's rule is that no image or sound appears which the child could not, at that age, actually perceive.

### Sight

| Age | What the eye can actually do |
|---|---|
| **Birth** | Visual acuity **20/400 to 20/600**. Vision beyond about one foot is blurred. The visual system is the least developed of all the senses at birth. Very sensitive to bright light. |
| **Birth – 2 months** | The **focal sweet spot is 8–12 inches** — roughly the distance from the breast to the mother's face, and nothing else. Colour is largely absent: for the first few weeks the world is essentially off the greyscale. High-contrast edges are the only strong signal. **The eyes often do not work together** for the first two months. |
| **~2 weeks** | **Red appears first.** It is the first colour to separate from grey. |
| **~6 weeks** | The **social smile** — the first smile evoked by a human face. The first evidence that the face is responding to *him*. |
| **2 months** | Colour vision begins expanding rapidly, red leading. |
| **3 months** | The eyes begin working together to focus and track. Tracking becomes smooth rather than jerky. |
| **4 months** | Near full-colour vision; many hues discriminable. |
| **~5 months** | **Depth perception arrives.** The eyes form a three-dimensional view for the first time. Not present at birth. |
| **5–8 months** | Reaching, recognising, recalling. Acuity around **20/40**, improving toward 20/25. |
| **8 months** | **Object permanence** — hidden things still exist. **Stranger wariness** appears (6–15 months). |
| **9–12 months** | Depth perception and distance judgement become reliable. |
| **18–24 months** | **Mirror self-recognition.** He understands the reflection is him. |
| **2 years** | Eye-hand coordination and depth perception well developed. |
| **3–5 years** | Adult 20/20 acuity arrives — it takes one to five more years after the main development finishes. |

Sources: [American Academy of Ophthalmology](https://www.aao.org/eye-health/tips-prevention/baby-vision-development-first-year) · [American Optometric Association](https://www.aoa.org/healthy-eyes/eye-health-for-life/infant-vision) · [newborn acuity data](https://www.lens.com/questions-answered/newborn-average-visual-acuity-development/) · [infant eyesight guide](https://millcreekeye.com/blog/when-can-babies-see-complete-guide-infant-eyesight)

### Hearing

| Age | What the ear can actually do |
|---|---|
| **Week 18** (in utero) | The ears pick up internal sounds — the mother's heartbeat, her body. |
| **Week 23** | Muffled sound from outside the womb becomes detectable, including the mother's voice, which resonates through her body straight to the fetal ear. |
| **Weeks 27–30** | The fetus turns its head or moves in response to outside sound. |
| **In utero, throughout** | The womb is a **low-pass filter**. This is the single most useful fact in the research: low-pass filtering strips the consonants and syllable structure out of speech, so that **prosody — rhythm, pitch, contour — becomes the primary acoustic element**. The child knows the *shape* of his mother's voice long before a single word. |
| **Birth** | Newborns **prefer the mother's voice** over a female stranger's, and can distinguish the father's voice from other male voices. The filter tears open: high frequencies arrive all at once. |
| **Early months** | Sound localisation is poor. The world is not yet a stereo field. |
| **Later** | Localisation sharpens; words begin separating out of undifferentiated speech noise. |

Sources: [MIT — prenatal sound shapes the auditory system](https://news.mit.edu/2022/prenatal-sound-womb-auditory-system-0525) · [low-pass filtering and prosody, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4364233/) · [hearing in the womb](https://lozierinstitute.org/dive-deeper/hearing-in-the-womb/) · [auditory milestones](https://www.healthpartners.com/blog/when-can-babies-hear-in-the-womb/)

### Mind

| Age | What the mind can actually do |
|---|---|
| ~6 weeks | Social smile |
| 6–12 months | Object permanence; a 6-month-old finds partly hidden things, a 9-month-old uncovers fully hidden ones |
| 6–15 months | Stranger wariness |
| 18–24 months | Mirror self-recognition — the beginning of a self |
| ~4 years | **Theory of mind** — understanding that other people have separate inner lives, their own thoughts and feelings |

Sources: [Lifespan Development — emotional and social development in infancy](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-hvcc-lifespandevelopment5/chapter/introduction-to-emotional-and-social-development-during-infancy/) · [Cognitive Development, NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537095/)

## 3. The story

A sensory reconstruction alone is a tech reel. This is what makes it a film.

**The film is about one face.**

From the first blurred oval at eight inches, the mother's face is the anchor of every act. The entire arc is a child learning to see her — first as a contrast edge, then as a colour, then as a shape at a distance, then as a person who responds, and finally, at four, **as someone with an inside.**

**The spine is a hand.** The film opens on the grasp reflex: a finger closed inside a fist, eight inches away, barely resolved. It ends on the same gesture inverted — a hand letting go at a school gate. Everything between is the distance the child travels between those two grips.

**The turn.** For three minutes the film is a beautiful, strange, wordless reconstruction of becoming able to perceive. Then theory of mind arrives, and the last minute is about something else entirely. At the school gate he lets go, walks, and turns back — and for the first time in his life, and the first time in the film, he understands that *she is sad.* Not that she is there. That she feels something, separately from him.

The POV, which has been a prison for four minutes, finally opens to include another mind. That is the resolution.

## 4. Structure — five acts, 4:00

| Act | Age | Time | The perceptual event | The story event |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I — The Blur** | Day 1 – Week 6 | 0:00–0:50 | 20/400. Greyscale. 8–12 inches. Eyes uncoupled. | A shape keeps returning. |
| **II — Red** | Week 6 – Month 4 | 0:50–1:35 | Red separates from grey. Tracking smooths. Eyes couple. | The shape smiles back. It is *for him.* |
| **III — Depth** | Month 4 – Month 12 | 1:35–2:30 | The world becomes three-dimensional. Full colour. | He can reach her. And she can be *gone*. |
| **IV — I Am Someone** | Year 1 – Year 2 | 2:30–3:10 | The POV rises off the floor. Mirror recognition. | He sees himself for the first time. |
| **V — Another Mind** | Year 3 – Year 5 | 3:10–4:00 | 20/20. Everything sharp. | He sees *her* for the first time. |

### Act I — The Blur (0:00–0:50)

Open in the womb: dark red luminance, a heartbeat at close range, a voice with all its consonants stripped away — pure prosody, the shape of speech without any speech. Then birth, and **the filter tears open.** High frequencies arrive all at once and it is violent. Light is unbearable — the newborn is genuinely hypersensitive to bright light. This is the film's one moment of real kinetic assault.

Then it settles. The world becomes a soft luminance field in which only high-contrast boundaries resolve: a hairline, the dark of an eye against skin, the edge of a jaw against a window. **Everything past a foot is nothing.** The frame drifts and occasionally doubles, because the eyes are not yet working together.

A shape arrives at eight inches. It leaves. It arrives again.

### Act II — Red (0:50–1:35)

**Red bleeds into a greyscale world first** — and it should be a specific red, chosen for the family: a thread on the wrist, a bindi, the warm coal of a gas ring, a plastic toy. Red exists for two weeks before anything else has colour, which is an extraordinary gift to a colourist and nobody else in the field will use it.

At six weeks, the social smile. The shape at eight inches changes, and the change is *caused by him.* First causality.

Then the other hues seep in, the edges firm, and tracking goes from jerky to smooth as the eyes learn to work together.

### Act III — Depth (1:35–2:30)

**The set piece.** At around five months the world snaps into three dimensions. Play it as the film's biggest single transition: a flat, pretty, painted world suddenly acquiring *distance*. Space becomes something you could put a hand into.

And it does. **His own hands enter frame** — the first time he sees a part of himself. He reaches for the face and can now judge how far away it is.

Then peek-a-boo, which is the object permanence beat and needs no explanation to any audience on earth. Her face vanishes behind her hands and, for the first time, he knows it is still there.

And then, at eight months, a face at eight inches that is *not hers* — and the world contains fear for the first time. Stranger wariness. Brief, sharp, and it re-values everything.

### Act IV — I Am Someone (2:30–3:10)

**The camera height changes.** For four minutes the POV has been on its back, then sitting. Now it stands, and the whole geometry of the world reorganises. Furniture becomes landscape. This is a purely cinematic event that costs nothing to write and lands enormously.

Then the mirror, at eighteen to twenty-four months. **We see him for the first time — in POV, as a reflection.** The formal reveal happens without breaking the rule. He touches the glass. The reflection touches back.

Meanwhile words begin separating out of undifferentiated speech noise, mirroring exactly what the image did in Act I. Sound resolves the same way light did.

### Act V — Another Mind (3:10–4:00)

Vision reaches 20/20. Everything is sharp, and — crucially — everything is now *ordinary*. The miraculous has become the everyday, which is its own quiet grief and the reason the film needs this act.

First day of school. Her hand. The gate.

He lets go. He walks. He turns back.

And the film does the only thing it has never done: **it holds.** On her. Long enough for him to notice her eyes, and her mouth, and the fact that she is holding something in. Four years old, and theory of mind has just arrived, and the first thing he uses it on is his mother.

He does not go back. He turns and goes inside. That is the film.

## 5. Visual grammar — the look bible

Rules to hold across every shot so the film reads as one designed object rather than a series of effects.

1. **The lens is the eye.** No cuts that a head could not make. Transitions are blinks, drifts, and the involuntary jerk of attention being captured. There is no editorial camera in this film until the final shot.
2. **Acuity is a parameter, and it only goes up.** Act I is 20/400; every act sharpens. Never sharpen and then soften again for effect — the whole film is one monotonic climb, and breaking that breaks the premise.
3. **Colour arrives in the researched order.** Greyscale, then red alone, then the rest. Never colour-grade an early act with a hue it has not earned.
4. **The 8–12 inch rule.** In Act I, anything past a foot is an unresolved field. Compose for a world that is one arm's length deep.
5. **Faces are partial.** Infants scan edges and high-contrast features rather than whole faces. Shoot cheeks, jawlines, a hairline, the dark of an eye. This is both accurate and the single biggest defence against face-drift artifacts.
6. **Light is the antagonist early.** The newborn is hypersensitive to bright light — blow out windows, let highlights bloom and hurt.
7. **The frame is unstable until the eyes couple.** Slight divergence and doubling through Act I, resolving around month three. Then it stops, permanently.
8. **Height is a clock.** Floor level, sitting height, standing height, and finally the height of a child looking back at an adult. The audience reads age from camera height without a single title card.

## 6. Sound design — the second film

The audio arc runs the identical curve to the visual one, and it is where a jury that includes serious craftspeople will notice whether the work is real.

| Stage | The mix |
|---|---|
| **Womb** | Everything low-pass filtered. Heartbeat close and constant. Voices reduced to pure prosody — rhythm and pitch contour with no consonants, no words, no intelligible syllables. |
| **Birth** | The filter tears open. Full frequency arrives instantly and harshly. This is the loudest, most violent moment in the film and it lasts a few seconds. |
| **Early months** | **No stereo field.** Sound is non-directional, because localisation is not there yet. Everything is present but nothing has a place. |
| **3–6 months** | The stereo field opens. Sounds acquire position. Heads turn toward them. |
| **~12 months** | Words begin separating out of speech noise — a slow resolve from babble to discrete, edged words. The auditory equivalent of Act III's depth. |
| **3–5 years** | Full clarity, full space, and the mix starts including things he never had room to notice before. |
| **Final beat** | At the gate, the mix drops almost everything and holds **her breath catching.** He can hear it because he can finally attend to someone who is not himself. |

Music, voice and sound design must all be AI-generated and uploaded into the submission project — that is a hard festival rule, and this film has an unusual amount of audio to build, so it is not a last-day task.

## 7. The series engine

Section 7 of the festival plan establishes that Higgsfield is buying **episode one of something**, not a closed short. This concept has a strong answer, and it may be a better one than the comedy format had.

**The format is an anthology of reconstructed consciousness.** Each episode rebuilds, with the same scientific rigour, what it is actually like inside another mind:

- A dog — dichromatic vision, a world organised by smell rendered visually, flicker-fusion differences.
- Someone losing their sight to retinitis pigmentosa, over a decade.
- A person with advancing dementia, where the failure is not perceptual but temporal.
- The last hour of a life.
- A person hearing through a cochlear implant for the first time.

Why this is a strong pitch to *this* buyer specifically: **every single episode is a different rendering problem.** The format is, structurally, a permanent showcase of the platform's range — and it is prestige-shaped rather than genre-shaped, which is exactly the gap in a slate currently full of *Dinoforce* and *Frozen Monster*. It is the kind of thing that gets a streaming service written about rather than merely watched.

And as with the previous concept, this episode resolves completely. Nothing is withheld.

## 8. Production notes

**The big advantage: there is almost no character consistency problem.** For roughly 95% of the runtime the protagonist is the camera. There is no hero face to lock, which removes the single most expensive and failure-prone part of AI filmmaking.

What *does* need locking:
- **The mother** — one face, needed clearly only from Act III onward. In Acts I and II she is scientifically required to be unresolvable, so drift is free.
- **The father** — a secondary presence, same logic.
- **The child** — needed for exactly two moments: the mirror at 18–24 months, and, if the alternate ending is used, the final shot.
- **The home** — one location family carried across five years, with set dressing aging rather than the architecture changing.

**Shot construction.** Build from long takes rather than many short ones: Seedance 2.5 generates up to 30 seconds in a single pass, and this film wants drifting, breathing, unhurried shots anyway. Ten to fourteen long takes will carry four minutes. That is the cheap way and the right way simultaneously.

**Cost.** The same ~7,500-credit estimate from the festival plan applies, possibly lower — heavy blur and low-contrast rendering in the first two acts should need fewer retries than sharp, detailed work.

**Risks, honestly:**
1. **Tedium.** Three minutes of soft imagery loses a room. Act I is capped at fifty seconds for exactly this reason, and the birth sequence has to be genuinely startling.
2. **Legibility at screening.** The first act must not read as an unfinished or abstract file. The returning face gives the screening team a narrative to hold on to within the first fifteen seconds.
3. **Sentimentality.** The material is one degree from a greeting card. The defence is rigour — the film earns its ending by being *accurate* for four minutes first, and by never using music to tell the audience what to feel until the final beat.
4. **Content rules.** Characters under 18 are permitted with restrictions, and no real person's face or voice may be used as an input — everything here is generated, so this is clean, but worth re-reading the rules before submitting.

## 9. What to publish today

Publishing a public project is what starts grant candidacy and Community Top 50 eligibility, and it needs only three things: **one scene, a poster, and a logline.**

- **Logline:** the one at the top of this document.
- **Poster:** a single frame from Act II — the red thread, alone in a grey world, slightly out of focus. It states the entire premise in one image and it is cheap to generate.
- **First scene:** the Act III depth transition, not Act I. Lead with the film's most spectacular moment rather than its most abstract one. The blur will read as intentional once viewers have seen what it resolves into; on its own, cold, it risks reading as a broken generation.
